Wisco the ICE Whisperer. A Voice for Shakopee

Audio + Transcript 28:23

As told by Wisco, who patrolled Shakopee through the Metro Surge. Recorded August 3, 2026, and published with his consent.

Read the full transcript

Brendan: So thank you for agreeing to talk to me about some of your stories. You were, in particular, one of the people that I wanted to interview most, because you were one of the most active people who were in Shakopee when all of the Metro Surge was going down — and also one of the most fun people to be out patrolling with, because you were always willing to be very confrontational with the assholes and just sort of… you really helped to reveal them to be clowns. And I think that was a lot of fun.

Wisco: They were, absolutely.

Brendan: And if you just want to tell us a bit about some of the more memorable experiences from those days.

Wisco: Oh, so Metro Surge had… there's so many stories that are flooding through my head right now. There's happy, positive stories about the people that I met — not ICE people, they're terrible, but the local people, the people we were protecting, the patrollers, all the food people. There's a million stories that are happy like that. And then there's the other side of the coin, where there's the confrontations with agents and all the different sightings and just the traumatic nature of it all.

I guess what I really wanted to talk about is just connecting it — especially with this being kind of a Shakopee audience — connecting it to various locations around town. Because to this day, I live on the east side of town over near Home Depot, kind of in the Southbridge area, and as I go about just my normal day to day, being in Shakopee, there's so many places that I go that trigger memories.

I went to Walmart yesterday, and every time I drive past Walmart or go to Walmart, I always look over at the order pickup area — kind of on the left side, if you're looking at the store. And I remember how we found an agent over by Panera, and I followed that agent around after he had his lunch at Panera. He kind of drove around town, got on 169 at Marshall, and I thought he was going to leave town and I was so excited to chase him out. And then he got off at County Road 21. And then we had a confrontation kind of in front of — between Sam's Club and Texas Roadhouse, in that little clinic cul-de-sac area there over by the urgent care.

And I expressed myself very verbally to him. Told him exactly what I thought of him, and all the hatred and the racism. I told him that he needed to leave Shakopee. I told him I did not approve of him hunting people for sport and recreation, and just how disgusting it was. And he did not care for that.

He then drove over by Walmart and parked in the order pickup area. And I followed him over there and I pulled up right next to him. And this was end of January — and I only remember that because it was that weekend where it was just stupid cold. It was that really long below-zero weekend.

Brendan: Where the like hundred thousand people came out and protested?

Wisco: Yeah, it was right around then.

And he went and parked over in the order pickup area, backed up into his spot, and I pulled up right next to him. And I immediately got out of my vehicle and started talking to him through the glass. And at that point my objective was to be calmer — I wanted to engage him in a conversation. So I talked to him through the glass and said, "I want to talk to you. I don't understand why you're doing what you're doing. I don't understand what motivates a person to have the job that you have."

And he started cracking his window down, and bit by bit the window came down a little more as we talked. I stood there in that cold and I talked to him for the better part of an hour. And the conversation itself was filled with the ICE propaganda of "we're going after the worst of the worst." And I said, "Oh, cool. So then you have judicial warrants." He's like, "No, we don't have judicial warrants." And we got into the debate of an administrative warrant versus a judicial warrant — the merits of one versus the other, and the legality of one versus the other.

And it turned into a conversation of me purposely just asking him questions and causing him to talk, because I was wasting his time. Because every minute that he was engaging with me was one less minute that he was out harassing people who didn't deserve it.

And it was a disgusting conversation. I literally threw up afterward, because talking to this guy was so gut-wrenching awful. It made me physically ill.

But every time I go to Walmart, I think about that guy. And I think about standing outside of his Ford Explorer — it was a black Ford Explorer with the super tinted windows. He had his window rolled down about halfway. And I remember how cold he was, even inside his car with the heater on. And he kept lowering his gaiter mask below his chin to talk to me, and then he would get cold and he would pull it back up. And you could see on the passenger seat of his car he had his tactical vest, with all his gear and weapons and all that.

And I think about that every time I go over there. I think about every time I drive through or drive near one of the apartment complexes on Marshall. And I think about a particular day that I was on patrol — I didn't see ICE in this particular encounter, but I was driving past an apartment complex where the units had garages.

And as I was driving past one of the units — it was late February, one of those nice late February days where, for February, it was warm, and people were kind of out, and they had the garage door of this unit open. And as I drove past, I saw an adult male who I presume is a father, and I saw three young girls. And they had beds in the garage covered in stuffed animals — pink and purple, color everywhere. It just screamed a little girl's room.

So they were sleeping out in the garage in the middle of February.

Brendan: And why do you think that they were there in that garage?

Wisco: I don't know them. I don't know their individual story. Knowing kind of the greater picture, my assumption is that they were in hiding. I don't know if it was their garage and they were just avoiding being in the house in case someone knocked on the door. I don't know if it was a friend's garage and they were just hanging out, staying away from their house. Perhaps it was a vacant garage and they were squatting.

I don't know what got them there, but my assumption is that they were in hiding because they were fearing for their safety — that ICE was coming for them next.

And even in February, even on a nice February day in Minnesota, it was cold. And there's no number of space heaters that you could have put into that garage that would have made it comfortable for a family to sleep in.

And I drive past that complex all the time. And every time I go past, I think of that family. I think of those little girls. I think about how startled they were that I drove past and they saw a white face go by — and the dad went to the wall and pushed the button to lower the door.

Brendan: I really remember those days when we were doing both patrols and food delivery, and just how self-conscious we always were doing food delivery — bringing the basic necessities of survival to the people who need them, because they're too afraid to leave their houses. How self-conscious we are about the fact that it's probably still quite terrifying to hear us just walking up the steps to their house. Or if we knock on the door, whether or not that's going to be a spike in your cortisol levels — because who knows whether that's people delivering your groceries as mutual aid, or if that's the government here to take you away.

Wisco: Right.

Brendan: I wonder if you'd be willing to talk about how much you find the memories of those days still affecting you today, as you drive past these locations that you're so familiar with and now have so many bad memories associated with.

Wisco: Yeah, it's still so triggering. My wife and I went on a date night, I don't know, a week or two ago, and we just decided to keep things pretty simple. We went over and had dinner at Turtle's. And driving just that little downtown strip of Shakopee, I remember an incident that I had where another patroller and I were sitting and watching an agent vehicle that was parked in front of Domino's.

And I think about just driving around town — especially now that it's summer and there's road construction and you get routed on so many of these weird routes during construction season. And my wife gets confused, because it's taking her down side roads and she's not familiar. And I'm like, "Yeah, just make a left up here." She's like, "Why do you know that?" I'm like, "Well, you'll learn a lot of these roads when you're out here patrolling." You discover side roads and back roads and alleys that you didn't know existed previously, but it was a good shortcut to get from this place to this place rather than going on a main road.

And like I said earlier, all over the city are reminders like that of — I saw an agent there, or agents were staking out a bus stop right off of Eagle Creek. All the places, all the different businesses where they were. The construction sites where families are living in those homes now, not realizing that the people who were building their homes were chased onto the roof in January, and the ladders got taken away by agents. And then eventually the workers got so cold that they volunteered to come down — because what choice did they have? They were going to freeze to death.

Or the kitchen staff at Ruby's — they got chased onto the roof in the same circumstance.

And just mentally, I walk around town and everywhere you remember all these stories. But the flip side to it is I also remember happy stories.

You were mentioning the food delivery and the trauma that could be induced of just us knocking on a door to deliver basic necessities. I will never forget the day where I did a delivery and I knocked on the door as I always did and I immediately turned to leave — and I had my son with me. And all of a sudden I heard the door behind me open, and I peeked over my shoulder, because I knew in my head that I couldn't have heard the door open — even though I know I heard it open.

And an elderly Hispanic woman, wearing a nightgown, came outside of her home, crying her eyes out, with her hands clasped together, just over and over again saying, "Gracias, gracias, muchas gracias."

And she came down the steps of her porch and gave me a hug. I've never met her before. I've never seen her before or since. But I will never forget the feeling of having this woman risk her safety to open the door, walk outside, and thank me for a delivery that in my heart was insufficient — because it was never going to be enough to feed the people of that home for a week.

And she was over the moon grateful. And I will never forget that.

Brendan: Kind of the way that ICE's presence during Metro Surge — a lot of people sort of imagined that it was very concentrated to just Minneapolis. And now that the news doesn't report on it so much, they sort of imagine that it's not happening anymore, even though it is happening here, and it's happening in many other states. And the public perception of it is a very self-contained thing, versus the reality when you're experiencing it as something that's so all-encompassing.

There isn't a place in Shakopee that someone doesn't have an ICE memory of.

And especially the way that you're talking about finding all these shortcuts — I distinctly remember how amazing it was, how much more I learned about Shakopee just going out and patrolling all the time, and how much more of a sense of place it gave me being out on those patrols. Just because I was spending all of my time getting to know this town so that we could protect it properly, and so that we could respond as quickly as possible when someone says, "Oh, there's ICE spotted over in this part of the neighborhood." And then we know where that neighborhood is and we just go right there. We know the fastest way to get to it without looking at a GPS.

Wisco: I was just going to say — you can instantly do that calculation of "I'm here, it's there," and you just kind of instant-GPS in your brain and you just know where to go.

Brendan: Yeah, exactly. And it's kind of shocking in a way, because I think that that's the way that we've lived as human beings forever, until we invented the GPS and then we lost that skill. And then something like this comes along and suddenly we have to relearn that skill, which you'd never expect.

Wisco: Yeah, it's a lot. It really… yeah, it's a lot.

And just one of the things that still sticks with me is just how long the activity has gone. I got involved in early January, and here we are beginning of August and it's still a thing. It's not flooding social media the way it was, but there are abductions around the Metro every day — whether it's people being unlawfully detained by police and put in lockup and then taken right out of the jail, or they're still doing smash-and-grabs around town.

And to the people who say, "Oh, Metro Surge ended, they left" — I mean, it's funny, but it's not funny, because they didn't leave. They just got quieter. And they learned from their mistakes of being loud and aggressive, and the public pushback that they received, and they adapted their tactics. And so now they're quieter.

And it's easy enough to give financial incentives to a sheriff's department, to have that sheriff pull someone over on some crap charge and put them in lockup for 48 hours on a hold. And then ICE comes in the middle of the night when there's nobody around to see it and just walks them right out the front door. It's just as effective for ICE — probably more so, because they're not surrounded by angry community and they don't get the bad press.

Brendan: Yeah. It's definitely the publicity and the public backlash is the only reason they bothered to look like they're withdrawing and to make their tactics a little bit less openly violent.

Do you feel like their tactics are still equally as violent, just more under the radar, as they were then?

Wisco: I think it depends where they're grabbing from. If they're taking someone out of a jail, the person's already in cuffs — there's really no need to be violent. Granted, there was never a need to be violent.

I think when they're grabbing people off the streets, or from gas stations, or pulling people over and pretending like they're law enforcement — I think those abductions are still plenty violent.

Brendan: So the way ICE operates, and the people who are operating within that organization, haven't changed. The attitude is still the same.

Wisco: Well, they keep shooting and killing people around the country. And when you look at the individuals that are involved in those incidents, they're people that were too stupid to be cops. They were too violent for the military, of all things. They have criminal records for violence.

I mean, it's straight out of a Saturday Night Live skit. "Are you too stupid to be a cop? Are you too fat to be in the army? Join ICE. We'll give you guns."

And that kind of shit is happening, and it's real. And then those people are put on the street with no training, and they get put into a stressful situation, and so they shoot people in the face.

Brendan: What always made me the angriest when Metro Surge was at its peak and they were consistently shooting people in the face every week — it's the way that so many Americans, but even Minnesotans, even people who are sitting here living in it directly, are able to turn a blind eye to it and pretend that it's not happening. Or worse, find every excuse under the sun to justify what's happening — to the point where it feels like they're preemptively trying to figure out the right combination of words that could give plausible deniability to the fact that what is very plainly just pure evil, happening directly in front of them, is actually just kind of acceptable.

And I felt like they're trying to do that because they know that if it is as evil as they know it to be, and if they acknowledge that, then that obligates them to do something about it. And therefore the only way that they can keep doing nothing about it — which is what they want to do — is to pretend that it's okay, and pretend that it's not as bad as they know it to be.

Wisco: Yeah. And I think for a lot of the people that are kind of at the heart of Metro Surge in terms of leadership and organization, this is the kind of moment that they've been waiting a long time for — where their hatred and their racism can be on full display and they don't need to hide it anymore. And they can finally accomplish those horrible and brutal goals that they've wanted to achieve for so long.

And they finally have all the right people in all the right places in power, where they can operate unchecked with a near-limitless budget. And the people in power who should be holding them accountable are giving them high fives and telling them to go do it again. And now the people who were holding them accountable are, for the most part, deluding themselves into thinking that it's over.

And I think that that's the part they've really been waiting for — is for the public resistance to die down.

Brendan: Well, and unfortunately the public is so used to terrible, horrible news that it's really easy for the public to go numb to something like this. When they were seeing videos from their neighborhood, their block, on social media — and it was the person that they knew, or knew by sight — there was outrage, and they responded to the outrage.

And then after a while it's kind of like mass shootings. There's a mass shooting almost every day in America. You don't get national news coverage for a mass shooting anymore, unless it's really over the top in comparison to other horrible mass shootings, because the public is just so used to it. It's like, "Oh, there was a mass shooting. Four people got murdered." "Oh, four. That's not so bad."

Like, that's a horrible sentiment. Four people lose their life and nobody even blinks an eye anymore.

Wisco: Yeah, I think that's the hardest thing to witness during times like these — is the devaluation of human life and individual suffering, and the way that we can just be so easily reduced to statistics. And the comparison of like, "Oh, this many millions of people died in the last century because of other ideologies, so compared to that, what's happening now that's objectively evil is actually relatively okay."

And the way that we use that sort of comparative behavior to justify atrocities — which will then, ironically, in the future be used as a comparison to justify future atrocities. And never seem to break out of that cycle.

Brendan: Yeah, it's absolutely insane. And there's enough voters who put the people in power that are doing this. And in my heart, I have to believe that some of them — hopefully a lot of them — regret their choices when they last visited the polling place, and they never thought it would get this bad. Or whatever the rationale is.

I'm desperately hoping that as we approach this next voting cycle — primary next week, general in November — I'm desperately hoping that people bring some humanity with them to the polling place, and remember that as we cast our votes, those votes have life-and-death consequences.

Wisco: Absolutely. And there's not an office on the ballot that doesn't have major, massive consequences with who wins — whether it's a judge, whether it's someone at the local level, the state level.

I always used to scoff at the ballot when I would look at some of these offices. I'm like, "I don't care who the state mine inspector is. I don't know what that job even does." I mean, they inspect mines, but what do they actually do?

But then when you roll it back and you realize how much corporate influence they have in their job — and with the strike of a pen, they can change the future of industry in an area. Well, all of a sudden that equates to thousands of jobs. And the ripples on the pond extend out to their families, and all the businesses that those families frequent. And all of a sudden that kind of humorous, inconsequential job on the ballot that nobody understands, all of a sudden really matters to a lot of families.

And I think that's where the power of the informed voter really makes a difference. And not just walking into the polling place and saying, "I'm going to vote for everyone with an R next to their name," or everyone with a D next to their name, or all the incumbents.

There needs to be thoughtful effort put into who you choose to vote for. Because every candidate for every office has an immense power and responsibility, and as they go about executing the duties of their office, it will impact you. And it may not always feel super direct, but it will catch up to you.

And if the people are elected — or reelected, whatever the case may be — who are going to do things that are going to negatively impact you, the only way… I shouldn't say the only way. The most effective way to push back on that is to exercise your rights at the ballot box.

Brendan: Very well said. And if that's not a call to action, then frankly I've never heard one before. And I think that that makes that a perfect place to leave off with this episode. So thank you very much.

Wisco: You're very welcome.

Brendan: It's not a podcast — but I know it feels like a podcast, doesn't it?

Wisco: It's a little bit of that format.

Brendan: I would very much like to have you on again and share some more stories. I know you have way more stories than I know about that you did this year.

Wisco: So I am always happy to talk to you.

Brendan: Fantastic. Well, thank you so much.

Share Your Story

If ICE has touched your life, your family, or your workplace here in Shakopee, your story matters — and you decide how it gets told.

Call or Text (763) 200-3711 Email brendanvanalstyne@gmail.com

What happens when you reach out

  1. You call, text, or email us — whatever's easiest. We'll set up a time to talk that works for you.
  2. We listen. In person, by phone, however you're comfortable. You tell us as much or as little as you want.
  3. You choose how it's shared: your own voice, a voice-altered recording, or written words with your name withheld. Nothing is decided for you.
  4. You review the final story — audio and transcript — before it ever goes on this site. You can change your mind at any point, for any reason.

We will never publish anything without your explicit, reviewed consent.
